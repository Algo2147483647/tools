# Fields, structures, energy and rigorous analysis

[Overview](../README.md) · [Event catalog and sources](./nineteenth-catalog.md) · [Personal names](../NAMES.md)

The nineteenth century cannot be read as physics merely borrowing a finished mathematical toolkit. Physical questions repeatedly changed mathematics. Joseph Fourier's heat problem required trigonometric representations of temperature distributions; disagreement about what counted as a function and when a series converged pushed analysis in new directions. The 1807 submitted memoir and the 1822 book are separate points. Later convergence and integration theory addressed issues that an effective formal calculation could leave unresolved.

The development of fields shows another interaction. Michael Faraday's experiments and physical interpretation did not begin as a complete set of equations. Potential theory and mathematical models helped translate experimental relationships into a more general framework. James Clerk Maxwell's field theory then predicted electromagnetic propagation, while Heinrich Hertz's experiments supplied laboratory evidence. The familiar compact vector form of the equations was a later reformulation, so the 1865 point should not be read as the appearance of every modern notation at once.

Energy and entropy emerged through different arguments. Work-heat measurements supported a conservation principle, whereas heat-engine analysis imposed restrictions on possible transformations. Rudolf Clausius's entropy was first a thermodynamic quantity. Statistical treatments by James Clerk Maxwell, Ludwig Boltzmann and Josiah Willard Gibbs related macroscopic behavior to distributions and microscopic arrangements. That connection required assumptions about statistics and dynamics; irreversibility is not obtained simply by reading time-reversible equations backward or forward.

Mathematics also expanded beyond the solution of particular physical problems. The impossibility of a general radical formula for quintics prompted structural questions about equations. Évariste Galois's permutation ideas, William Rowan Hamilton's quaternions, Hermann Grassmann's extension algebra and Arthur Cayley's matrices broadened the kinds of algebraic object under investigation. Some later became central to physics, but their eventual usefulness should not be mistaken for a predetermined original aim.

Geometry underwent a comparable change. Николай Лобачевский and Bolyai János developed alternatives to the usual parallel postulate. Bernhard Riemann then proposed a broad local metric viewpoint. His 1854 lecture was published only in 1868, and its gravitational application came much later. The line to general relativity marks a subsequent mathematical use, not a claim that the lecture already contained the physical theory.

Foundational questions grew sharper as infinite processes became routine. Richard Dedekind's construction of real numbers, Georg Cantor's distinctions between infinities and formal logical work supplied new ways of stating what a mathematical argument assumes. Meanwhile, increasingly sensitive instruments exposed phenomena that existing physical models struggled to explain: cathode rays, X-rays and radioactive emissions. The century ends with new objects of investigation rather than with a completed, untroubled classical physics.
