# Logic computation and information

This strand asks what can be represented and computed, and how explanations can be compared. Leibniz is an intellectual antecedent; Boole and Frege supply formal structure; Church and Turing define effective computation. Godel and complexity theory qualify what a formal system or finite computation can accomplish. None of these results makes human cognition automatically computable in practice, or proves that it is not.

Information and description length form a second branch. Shannon studies communication; Solomonoff, Kolmogorov and Rissanen connect programs, compression and induction. This branch reaches theoretical general intelligence through AIXI and intelligence measures. Its practical descendants use approximations, so ideal results should not be silently transferred to finite models.

Follow the cross-links into neural networks, probabilistic learning and AGI theory. The important distinction is between an expressive computational substrate, an inference principle and an efficiently trained intelligent system.
